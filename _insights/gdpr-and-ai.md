# GDPR and AI: what small businesses actually need to worry about

<div class="insight-meta" style="margin-bottom: 1.5rem;">
  <span>6 min read</span>
  <span class="dot"></span>
  <span>Updated August 2026</span>
</div>

If you're a small business using AI tools that touch customer data — like email, inquiries, or reviews — your two real obligations are knowing exactly where that data flows, and disclosing when a message was generated automatically without a human reviewing it first. As of August 2026, that second point is not optional. It's enforced under the EU AI Act's Article 50 transparency rules, with fines running up to €15 million or 3% of global turnover for serious violations. Most of GDPR compliance for a small business comes down to documentation and honesty, not complicated legal work.

## The two things that actually apply to you

First, know where your data flows: which tools touch it, and whether there's a written data processing agreement with any AI vendor involved. Second, disclose when content was generated and sent automatically without a human reviewing it first. Everything else in GDPR mostly follows from getting these two right and writing them down.

## Controller vs. processor, in plain language

You, the business, are the controller of your own customer data — the emails, names, and inquiries that belong to your business. A studio or tool automating something on your behalf is a processor, acting only on your documented instructions and only for the purpose you agreed to. A processor worth trusting won't use your data for anything beyond that, won't sell it, and will tell you exactly which sub-processors, like an AI provider, ever touch it.

## What the EU AI Act adds in 2026

The Act's Article 50 transparency rules became enforceable on August 2, 2026. They apply to any AI system that interacts with people or generates content people see, chatbots included. A chatbot has to identify itself as a machine, at or before the very first interaction. This obligation sits with the business using the tool, not just the company that built it — so it applies even if you're using someone else's AI platform rather than something custom-built.

## What good practice actually looks like

Keep customer data inside your own accounts rather than exporting it to unrelated third parties. Get documentation, in writing, of exactly what data flows where before anything goes live. Add a short disclosure line to any message that's generated and sent automatically without a human reviewing it first, like an instant lead reply or a review request. Keep a human reviewing and sending anything more sensitive, which removes the disclosure requirement for that message and keeps you in control regardless.

## What this doesn't mean

It doesn't mean you need a data protection officer or a legal team if you're a small operation handling ordinary business contact data. It doesn't mean you can't use AI. And it doesn't mean every message needs a disclosure — only the ones sent automatically without a human reviewing them first. Most of this is achievable with clear documentation and a studio or vendor willing to put it in writing.
